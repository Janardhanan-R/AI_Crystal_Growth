Uncertainty-Aware Machine Learning Surrogates and Human-in-the-Loop Decision Support for Melt-Crystal Interface Control in Vertical Bridgman Crystal Growth

R. Anandha Sree <anandhasreer.cse@sathyabama.ac.in>
Janardhanan Ramesh <janardhananrameshh@gmail.com>

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ABSTRACT
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Melt-crystal interface control is central to producing high-quality single crystals via the vertical Bridgman (VB) method, yet interface position and shape are difficult to observe or adjust in situ, and the physics-based numerical simulations used to study them are computationally expensive. This paper presents an uncertainty-aware machine learning framework for surrogate modeling and decision support in a five-heater VB furnace growing BaBrCl crystal, using the publicly available, CrysMAS simulation-generated dataset of 96 operating points introduced by the only prior machine-learning study on this system; this original dataset was not generated by the authors of the present paper. Where that study compared two algorithms on two pooled output quantities, we independently model the centerline and edge interface positions (L_c, L_e) and their deflection (D) across seven regression algorithms under a leakage-free, five-fold cross-validation protocol, selecting each target's model using a cross-validation-only, multi-metric ranking that never consults the held-out test set. Gaussian process regression (GPR) achieved the strongest overall performance across the three targets in the evaluated setup, achieving test-set R^2=0.996, R^2=0.987, and R^2=0.617 for L_c, L_e, and D respectively, while additionally providing predictive uncertainty estimates, which are not reported in the prior studies reviewed here. We identify and partially resolve a methodological discrepancy with the reference study's reported deflection performance by introducing a pooled cross-validation statistic directly comparable to theirs, and we compare our SHAP-derived feature rankings against the reference study's own SHAP findings, observing agreement on the leading heater variables associated with interface deflection. Beyond single-point inverse design, we extend the reference study's manual, four-scenario heuristic check into a systematic decision-support pipeline: a virtual candidate exploration module surrogate-evaluates large batches of heater set-point combinations, screens them against physical range, ordering, and convex-hull support constraints, and ranks them by predicted deflection and predictive uncertainty, producing a flagged, multi-view candidate table intended for review by a domain expert prior to any CrysMAS re-simulation or physical trial; these 1,000 virtual candidates are evaluated only by the trained surrogate model and have not been validated using new CrysMAS simulations or physical experiments. This paper reports only results and figures produced by code executed as part of this study; no experimental validation, physical trial, or third-party performance figure is claimed beyond what is cited from the reference study. The code, trained models, and result tables developed for this study are documented here for reproducibility and are available as described in the Code and Data Availability statement.

Keywords: machine learning, crystal growth, vertical Bridgman method, surrogate modeling, Gaussian process regression, explainable AI, SHAP, inverse design, candidate exploration, uncertainty quantification, decision support, BaBrCl

I. INTRODUCTION
==============================================================================
The vertical Bridgman (VB) method remains one of the most widely used techniques for growing bulk single crystals from the melt [1]. As the ampoule is translated from the hot zone toward the cold zone, a melt-crystal interface separates the still-molten and already-solidified material. The macroscopic shape and position of this interface is a first-order determinant of the final crystal's quality: an interface that is moderately convex toward the melt minimizes thermoelastic stress and suppresses the nucleation of cracks and dislocations, while an interface that drifts or deforms during growth invites defect formation and non-uniform impurity distribution [2]. Because the interface forms deep inside an opaque, high-temperature furnace, it cannot be observed directly during production, and adjusting it requires indirect control through the power delivered to a bank of independently controlled heaters.

Physics-based numerical simulation - typically finite-volume or finite-element heat-transfer models such as CrysMAS - has long been used to study how heater set-points shape the interface, and such models have been validated against experimental and neutron-imaging measurements for the BaBrCl system considered in this paper [4]. However, these simulations are slow, are typically operated in a forward direction only (set-points -> interface shape), and offer no native mechanism for solving the inverse problem of recommending set-points that achieve a desired interface outcome, nor for helping a process engineer compare many candidate operating points at once. This has motivated a growing body of work applying machine learning (ML) models, trained on simulation output, as fast surrogates for crystal-growth process design [8]. Most such surrogates, however, are neural-network black boxes that offer no insight into why a prediction was made, report a single point recommendation instead of a reviewable set of options, and rarely report predictive uncertainty estimates - a serious omission for any model intended to inform real process decisions, not replace the engineer who must ultimately act on them.

The most directly relevant prior work is Zhang and Cheng [2], who trained linear regression (LR) and random forest (RF) models on a 96-point CrysMAS-simulated design of experiments for a five-heater VB furnace growing BaBrCl crystal, and used SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values to explain the RF model. Their study is a valuable proof of concept for explainable ML in crystal growth, but it has several limitations that we address directly in this paper: (i) it models only two pooled output quantities - average interface position P and deflection D - in place of the physically distinct centerline and edge positions that determine them; (ii) it compares only two algorithms, neither of which reports predictive uncertainty; (iii) its own reported cross-validation statistic is ambiguous with respect to how out-of-fold predictions are aggregated, complicating exact reproduction; and (iv) its "application to crystal growth practice" is a manual exercise - reading a SHAP plot by eye, proposing four candidate heater adjustments by hand, and checking them against scenarios already present in the training data - in place of a systematic, reviewable decision-support workflow.

This paper makes the following contributions:

1. We independently model the centerline interface position L_c, edge interface position L_e, and interface deflection D from the five heater set-points, comparing seven regression algorithms (linear regression, ridge regression, support vector regression, random forest, XGBoost, histogram-based gradient boosting, and Gaussian process regression) under an identical, leakage-free five-fold cross-validation protocol, with model selection performed strictly from cross-validation results and never from the held-out test set.
  2. We introduce a pooled cross-validation statistic, computed identically to what the reference study appears to report, alongside the conventional mean-of-folds statistic, enabling a direct, like-for-like comparison of our results to theirs and resolving a substantial fraction of an initially observed performance discrepancy.
  3. We package the selected models as a fast, uncertainty-aware surrogate that reports predictive uncertainty estimates via Gaussian process regression - a capability absent from all prior machine-learning work on this system - together with input/output range validation that flags extrapolation.
  4. We compute SHAP explanations for our models and perform, to our knowledge, the first cross-study comparison of SHAP-derived feature rankings in this domain, observing agreement with the reference study's SHAP rankings on the leading heater variables associated with interface deflection, while showing that engineered thermal-gradient features carry signal beyond the raw set-points used in prior work.
  5. We replace the reference study's single manual heater-adjustment procedure with an automated, uncertainty-penalized inverse-design optimizer that searches for heater set-points achieving a specified interface outcome, subject to the physical ordering constraint required for directional solidification.
  6. We extend single-point optimization into a virtual candidate exploration module that surrogate-evaluates large batches of heater set-point combinations, screens them against physical support constraints, and ranks them by predicted outcome and predictive uncertainty, producing a structured, multi-view output table designed for human-in-the-loop review, not for autonomous deployment; every ranked candidate is evaluated only by the trained surrogate model and is not validated using new CrysMAS simulations or physical experiments.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews related work. Section III describes the dataset and problem formulation. Section IV details the complete methodology, including the candidate exploration and decision-support pipeline. Section V presents the system architecture. Section VI presents and discusses results. Section VII states limitations, and Section VIII concludes.

==============================================================================
II. RELATED WORK
==============================================================================
Physics-based simulation of Bridgman and related melt-growth processes has a long history, from early one-dimensional analytical thermal models [7] to modern finite-volume codes such as CrysMAS that resolve two-dimensional, axis-symmetric temperature fields under conductive and radiative heat transfer [6]. Such simulations have been validated against physical measurements of BaBrCl and BaBrCl:Eu crystals grown in VB furnaces, including via energy-resolved neutron imaging [4], giving confidence that simulation-trained models capture behavior representative of the real process.

Machine learning has increasingly been applied on top of such simulations to accelerate crystal-growth process design, including predicting interface shape and position [9], growth rate [10], furnace temperature fields [11], and melt flow [12]. The large majority of these studies employ artificial neural networks [8], which achieve strong predictive accuracy but function as black boxes, offering no direct insight into which process variables drive a given prediction - a serious limitation when the goal is to guide, not merely predict, manufacturing decisions.

Explainable machine learning addresses this gap by attaching interpretable structure to otherwise opaque models. SHAP, grounded in cooperative game theory, quantifies each input feature's contribution to each individual prediction and has been shown to recover globally consistent, locally faithful explanations for tree-ensemble models [13,14]. Zhang and Cheng [2] were, to our knowledge, among the first to apply SHAP specifically to a crystal-growth prediction problem, explaining a random forest model of interface position and deflection for the same BaBrCl VB system and dataset used in this paper. Our work is a direct methodological extension of theirs, detailed model-by-model in Section IV and compared quantitatively in Section VI.

A separate, largely distinct line of work in manufacturing and materials informatics has argued that autonomous point-recommendation is often less useful in practice than decision support: presenting a domain expert with a small, ranked, uncertainty-flagged set of options instead of a single unexplained answer [8]. This paper's candidate exploration module (Sections IV-I-IV-K) is designed in this spirit. We do not claim to have conducted a human-factors or expert-review study; rather, we report the design and output of a screening pipeline intended to support, and not replace, expert judgement.

III. DATASET AND PROBLEM FORMULATION
==============================================================================

III-A. Furnace and Dataset
------------------------------------------------------------
The dataset used in this study is a CrysMAS simulation-generated dataset of 96 operating points for a five-heater VB furnace growing BaBrCl crystal, an alkaline-halide scintillator material of interest for medical and security applications [5]. This original dataset was produced by numerical simulation in the reference study and made available via Mendeley Data [3,2]; we did not generate this dataset ourselves. We obtained the published dataset and used it as the basis for the independent machine-learning experiments - model training, evaluation, explainability analysis, and the surrogate-based candidate exploration described later in this paper - that constitute our contribution. The furnace's five heaters - top, upper, middle, lower, and bottom - are independently controlled to set-point temperatures T_1,...,T_5 respectively, each measured in kelvin at a thermocouple positioned at the heater's mid-height. For each combination of set-points, CrysMAS numerically solves the steady-state heat-diffusion equation under radiative and conductive heat transfer to determine the temperature field, from which the melt-crystal interface - the isotherm at BaBrCl's melting point, T_mp=1168K - is extracted [2].

Two positions on this interface are recorded: L_c, its position at the ampoule centerline (r=0), and L_e, its position at the ampoule wall (r=6.5mm). From these, two derived quantities are also reported: the average position P and the deflection D, related to L_c and L_e by the exact identities

P = (Lc + Le) / 2 D = Lc - Le

with the equivalent inverse relationship L_c = P + D/2 and L_e = P - D/2. A positive deflection, D>0, indicates a convex interface (favorable, lower thermal stress), while D<0 indicates a concave interface.

A full-factorial design over the levels shown for each heater in the source furnace design would yield 3^3 x 2^2=108 combinations; the physical requirement that T_1>= T_2>= T_3>= T_4>= T_5 - a positive axial temperature gradient, necessary to drive directional solidification downward - eliminates 12 infeasible combinations, leaving n=96 simulated operating points, each with five inputs and four recorded quantities (L_c, L_e, P, D).

To avoid any ambiguity in what follows, we distinguish explicitly between three distinct bodies of data used or produced in this paper: (1) the original, numerically simulated 96-point CrysMAS dataset described above, which was generated by the reference study and which we did not generate ourselves; (2) our own machine-learning experiments - model training, cross-validation, testing, and explainability analysis - performed using that 96-point dataset; and (3) the 1,000 virtual candidate heater set-point combinations introduced in Section IV-I, which are sampled and evaluated only by our trained surrogate model and have never been run through CrysMAS or tested physically. These three items are not interchangeable, and results derived from one should not be read as validating another.

III-B. Target Selection and Data Verification
------------------------------------------------------------
Before any modeling was performed, we verified programmatically that Eq. (1) holds exactly across all 96 rows (maximum absolute deviation on the order of 10^-17, consistent with floating-point round-off). This confirms that P carries no information beyond L_c and L_e and is therefore excluded from modeling as redundant, while D, although algebraically derivable from L_c and L_e, is modeled directly as an independent regression target - consistent with the reference study's own treatment of deflection as a primary output of interest, and because a model trained directly on D is not forced to inherit compounding error from two upstream predictions. This verification step is reported here explicitly because rigorous confirmation of a dataset's documented column semantics against the data itself, prior to any modeling, is a basic but frequently skipped element of reproducible ML research.

We therefore model L_c, L_e, and D as three regression targets, each a function of the five heater set-points T_1,...,T_5.

==============================================================================
IV. METHODOLOGY
==============================================================================

IV-A. Preprocessing and Leakage Prevention
------------------------------------------------------------
The dataset contains no missing values and no duplicate rows; every row corresponds to a distinct combination of heater set-points. An interquartile-range (IQR) screen identifies one to three rows per target as statistical outliers, but inspection shows these correspond to the most extreme corner points of the temperature design, not measurement or simulation error, and they are retained instead of removed. All modeling uses scikit-learn [19] Pipeline objects that couple a StandardScaler feature-standardization step to each estimator, so that during cross-validation the scaler is fit only on each training fold and never observes validation or test data - preventing the common leakage failure mode of standardizing before splitting.

IV-B. Feature Engineering
------------------------------------------------------------
In addition to the five raw set-points, we construct six physically motivated derived features: the four adjacent-zone axial gradients Delta T_i,i+1=T_i-T_i+1 for i=1,...,4; the total axial temperature drop Delta T_15=T_1-T_5; and the mean furnace temperature Tbar=15sum_i=1..5T_i. These eleven features (five raw, six engineered) were chosen because axial gradients and overall thermal level are the physically relevant quantities governing heat flow and interface shape in a Bridgman furnace, and because our SHAP analysis (Section VI-E) subsequently confirms that several of these engineered features carry explanatory signal exceeding that of the raw set-points from which they are derived. The identical engineered-feature pipeline is reused, unmodified, by the candidate exploration module (Section IV-I) so that virtual candidates are evaluated in exactly the same feature space the surrogate was trained on.

IV-C. Candidate Models
------------------------------------------------------------
Table 1 lists the seven regression algorithms compared, each wrapped in an identical scaling pipeline and evaluated under identical cross-validation folds for a fair, controlled comparison. Random forest and the boosting model (XGBoost, or a documented substitute, see Section VI-D) were each tuned once via three-fold grid search on the training split only, with the resulting hyperparameters held fixed across all subsequent five-fold comparisons - a deliberate simplification relative to nested cross-validation, justified by instability of nested search at n approx 77 training rows, and stated here explicitly as a limitation rather than concealed.

Table 1. Candidate Regression Models
  Model                          | Role in comparison
  -------------------------------|--------------------------------------------
  Linear Regression               | Interpretable linear baseline
  Ridge Regression                | Regularized linear baseline
  SVR (RBF kernel)                | Nonlinear kernel baseline

  XGBoost [17]                    | Regularized gradient-boosted trees

  Gaussian Process Reg. [18]      | Nonlinear surface + predictive uncertainty

IV-D. Cross-Validation and Evaluation Protocol
------------------------------------------------------------
The dataset is split 80/20 into training (n=77) and held-out test (n=19) subsets with a fixed random seed. All hyperparameter tuning and model selection use only the training subset; the test subset is touched exactly once, after selection, purely as an independent check. Within the training subset, five-fold cross-validation (K=5, shuffled, fixed seed) is used for both hyperparameter tuning and model comparison. Group- or time-aware splitting was considered and rejected, since every row is an independent design point with no repeated trials, batches, or temporal ordering to leak across folds.

For each model m and target y in L_c,L_e,D, we report two distinct summary statistics, deliberately kept separate because they can diverge materially on a small, noisy target:

Mean-of-folds. For each fold k=1,...,5, MAE, RMSE, and R^2 are computed on that fold's held-out predictions, and the five per-fold scores are averaged, with their standard deviation reported as a stability measure:

MAE_mean = (1/5) * sum over folds k=1..5 of MAE_k, where MAE_k = (1/n_k) * sum over i in fold k of |y_i - y_hat_i| (and RMSE, R^2 are computed analogously per fold, then averaged)

and analogously for RMSE and R^2. This statistic is used for model selection (Section IV-E) because its standard deviation quantifies fold-to-fold stability.

Pooled. All five folds' out-of-fold predictions are instead combined into a single array of n=77 predictions (via cross_val_predict on the identical fold assignment), and a single MAE, RMSE, and R^2 are computed once over the pooled set:

R^2_pooled = 1 - [ sum_i=1..n (y_i - y_hat_i)^2 ] / [ sum_i=1..n (y_i - ybar)^2 ]

This statistic is reported in addition to the mean-of-folds statistic specifically because it is what the reference study [2] appears to report - its Figure 2 plots all 96 out-of-fold predictions in a single scatter with one R^2/MAE annotation - and reporting both is what enables the direct, like-for-like comparison in Section VI-C.

IV-E. Model Selection Protocol
------------------------------------------------------------
For each target, the seven models are ranked separately by mean-of-folds MAE, RMSE, and R^2 (each equally weighted), and the model with the lowest average rank is selected. Selection uses exclusively the mean-of-folds cross-validation statistic on the training split; neither the pooled statistic nor the held-out test set is ever consulted for selection, eliminating both look-ahead bias and the risk of selecting a model that merely fits one particular fold assignment well.

IV-F. Independent versus Multi-Output Modeling
------------------------------------------------------------
Random forest (natively multi-output) and the boosting model (via MultiOutputRegressor) were each additionally trained to predict L_c, L_e, and D jointly, and compared under identical cross-validation folds against the average of their independently trained per-target counterparts. The joint and independent variants performed comparably (Section VI-G); independent per-target models were retained for the final surrogate because they permit a different algorithm to be selected per target, which materially affected the outcome of this study.

IV-G. Fast Surrogate Deployment
------------------------------------------------------------
The selected model per target is refit on the complete 96-row dataset (training and test combined, since both were already used to select and validate the modeling approach) and wrapped in a deployable surrogate class that: (i) reports point predictions in well under one millisecond per sample; (ii) reports a predictive standard deviation wherever the selected model is a Gaussian process; (iii) flags, without blocking, any query whose inputs fall outside the training data's observed range (extrapolation); and (iv) flags any prediction falling far outside the observed training range for that target. The surrogate is explicitly framed as a data-driven approximation of the CrysMAS simulation, not a physics-based or hybrid digital twin, since no governing heat-transfer equations are encoded in its own logic. This single surrogate object is reused, without retraining, by every downstream component described in the remainder of this section.

IV-H. Explainability
------------------------------------------------------------
SHAP TreeExplainer is applied to the fitted random forest model for each target, computed in the same standardized feature space the model was trained on, to produce per-feature, per-sample attribution values. Random forest was chosen as the explained model specifically because it is the model the reference study also explains via SHAP, enabling the direct cross-study comparison reported in Section VI-E.

IV-I. Automated, Uncertainty-Aware Inverse Design
------------------------------------------------------------
Given a target outcome y^* (a specified value, or combination of values, for L_c, L_e, and/or D), we search for heater set-points T=(T_1,...,T_5) that achieve it by minimizing the objective

J(T) = (target error term) + lambda_u * mean_predictive_variance(T) + lambda_c * (ordering-violation penalty) target error term = sum over requested targets k of (y_hat_k(T) - y*_k)^2 mean_predictive_variance(T) = mean of sigma_k(T)^2 over requested targets ordering-violation penalty = sum_{i=1..4} max(0, T_(i+1) - T_i)^2

where y_hat_k(T) and sigma_k(T) are the surrogate's predicted mean and standard deviation (where available) at candidate set-point T, mean(sigma^2(T)) is the mean predictive variance over the requested targets, and the final term heavily penalizes any candidate violating the physical ordering constraint T_1>= T_2>= T_3>= T_4>= T_5 required for directional solidification [2]. The uncertainty term actively discourages the optimizer from recommending set-points the surrogate itself is unsure about, instead of extrapolating with false confidence. Because tree-ensemble and Gaussian-process surrogates are not differentiable in closed form, J is minimized with differential evolution [15], a gradient-free global optimizer, with the search space bounded by default to the training data's observed set-point ranges. This single-target optimizer returns exactly one recommended set-point; Sections IV-I-IV-K describe how this is extended into a multi-candidate decision-support workflow.

IV-J. Virtual Candidate Exploration
------------------------------------------------------------
Instead of searching for a single optimum, the candidate exploration module samples a large batch of heater set-point combinations T^(1),...,T^(N) (default N=1000, configurable), drawn either by uniform random sampling or, optionally, Latin hypercube sampling, with each T_i bounded to the observed training range for that heater. Rejection sampling discards any draw violating the physical ordering constraint T_1>=...>= T_5, and near-duplicate candidates are removed by rounding to a configurable decimal precision. If fewer than N valid candidates are obtained after sampling, the module records the shortfall explicitly instead of silently padding the output with repeated or synthetic rows. Each retained candidate is passed through the identical feature-engineering step used in training (Section IV-B) and evaluated only by the fixed, already-trained surrogate model (Section IV-G) - no retraining occurs, and none of these virtual candidates is validated using new CrysMAS simulations or physical experiments: no ground-truth CrysMAS value is available or used for these virtual points, since they were never simulated or physically tested.

IV-K. Constraint- and Uncertainty-Based Candidate Filtering
------------------------------------------------------------
Each evaluated candidate is checked against three independent support criteria: (i) whether every T_i falls within the observed per-heater training range; (ii) whether the physical ordering constraint is satisfied; and (iii) whether the candidate's five-dimensional set-point vector lies inside the convex hull of the 96 training points, computed via Delaunay triangulation. A candidate is assigned a support_category of in_range only if all three checks pass; otherwise it is labeled outside_convex_hull, outside_range, or invalid_constraint, in that priority order. No candidate is ever described as physically validated on the basis of these checks alone - they establish only that a candidate is not a known extrapolation with respect to the training design, not that CrysMAS or experiment would confirm the surrogate's prediction at that point.

For each candidate, we additionally compute the surrogate's per-target predictive uncertainty and an aggregate_uncertainty score: the mean, across L_c, L_e, and D, of each target's predictive standard deviation after min-max normalization within the current candidate set (so this score is a relative ranking valid only within one exploration run, not an absolute or cross-run quantity). Candidates whose aggregate uncertainty exceeds a configurable percentile of the candidate set's own distribution (default: the 90th percentile) are flagged high_uncertainty, again as a caution flag for review, not an exclusion criterion.

A documented, deliberately simple multi-objective screening score is then computed for each in-range candidate,

s = w_D * |D|_normalized + w_u * u_normalized + w_c * p (w_D = 0.5, w_u = 0.3, w_c = 0.2 by default; p = 0 or 1 support penalty)

where |D| and u are the candidate's absolute predicted deflection and aggregate uncertainty, each min-max normalized within the candidate set; p in 0,1 is a binary support penalty; and the default weights are w_D=0.5, w_u=0.3, w_c=0.2. These weights are stated explicitly as a transparent, configurable choice, not a scientifically derived or physically optimal weighting. Lower s indicates a candidate that is simultaneously closer to a flat interface, better supported by the training data, and less uncertain.

IV-L. Human-in-the-Loop Decision Support
------------------------------------------------------------
The filtered, scored candidate set is exported as three artifacts: the full evaluated candidate table, a top-10 table ranked by absolute deflection, and a machine-readable summary of run configuration and support-category counts. In addition to ranking by |D|, the module supports ranking by aggregate uncertainty and by the combined screening score, giving a reviewer several complementary views of the same candidate set instead of a single collapsed answer. Every exported row carries its support flags and uncertainty values alongside its predictions, so that a domain expert reviewing the table can see not only what the surrogate predicts but how much to trust that prediction and why a candidate was or was not screened out. This is the paper's human-in-the-loop decision-support contribution: a structured, transparent, multi-view candidate table designed for expert review before any CrysMAS re-simulation or physical trial. We emphasize, to avoid any overstatement, that this paper reports the design and the surrogate-side output of this workflow; it does not report the results of an independent human expert review of these candidates, which was not conducted as part of this study.

V. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
==============================================================================
Figure 1 summarizes the full pipeline as a single data-flow diagram, distinguishing the computational stages implemented and executed in this study from the downstream human-in-the-loop workflow that this study proposes but does not itself carry out. The pipeline has three stages. In the modeling stage, the raw dataset is preprocessed, engineered into eleven features, and used to train and cross-validate seven candidate regression algorithms per target; the best-performing model per target (Section VI-B) is selected by cross-validation alone and refit as the deployed surrogate, which reports both a point prediction and, where the selected model is a Gaussian process, a predictive standard deviation. In the search stage, the fixed surrogate is queried in two complementary ways without ever being retrained: the automated inverse-design optimizer (Section IV-H) searches for a single uncertainty-penalized optimum for a specified target outcome, while the virtual candidate exploration module (Section IV-I) evaluates a large batch of sampled set-point combinations in parallel. Candidates from the exploration branch pass through constraint- and support-based filtering (Section IV-J) before both branches converge into a single ranked, uncertainty-flagged output - a recommendation prepared for human expert review, not a recommendation that has already been reviewed. The subsequent decision stage - review by a human domain expert, and any resulting CrysMAS re-simulation or physical furnace trial - is shown in Figure 1 as the intended next step in this workflow. We emphasize that no human expert review, CrysMAS re-simulation, or physical trial of these outputs was conducted as part of this study; these steps remain future work (Section VIII). In Figure 1, solid-outline boxes correspond to computational components implemented and executed in this study, while dashed-outline boxes correspond to the intended human-in-the-loop workflow - expert review and physical or simulation-based validation - neither of which has been performed here.

VI. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
==============================================================================
All results below were produced with fixed random seeds (data split, cross-validation shuffling, and every stochastic model fixed at seed 42) and are fully reproducible from the underlying dataset and code described in the Code and Data Availability statement.

VI-A. Cross-Validation and Test-Set Performance
------------------------------------------------------------
Table 2 summarizes five-fold cross-validation R^2 for all seven models on all three targets, together with the pooled-CV R^2 for the deflection target D (the statistic directly comparable to the reference study, discussed in Section VI-C). Gaussian process regression achieves the best cross-validation performance on every target, by a wide margin on L_c and L_e and by a smaller but still decisive margin on D.

On the held-out test set, the selected Gaussian process surrogate achieves R^2=0.996 for L_c, R^2=0.987 for L_e, and R^2=0.617 for D. The held-out results are broadly consistent with the cross-validation results, although the small test-set size (approximately 19 samples, from the 96-row dataset's 80/20 split) limits the strength of conclusions that can be drawn about generalization; we therefore do not claim that overfitting has been ruled out, only that no strong overfitting signal is observed at this sample size. Full per-model, per-target MAE and RMSE tables (21 modelxtarget combinations, omitted here for space) are provided in the accompanying repository.

Figure 2 compares actual against predicted values for D, the harder of the two interface positions to predict, across all seven models on the held-out test set. The Gaussian process panel (top left) is visibly the tightest fit to the diagonal, consistent with its highest test-set R^2; the corresponding plots for L_c and L_e, where every model achieves R^2>0.86 and GPR again achieved the strongest performance among the evaluated models, are provided in the repository and omitted here for space. Figure 3 shows the corresponding residual (actual minus predicted) distribution for D: the Gaussian process residuals are concentrated close to zero relative to the weaker models, with no strong systematic bias. Figure 4 shows the selected GPR surrogate's 95% predictive interval for each test sample, sorted by actual value; the true value falls inside or close to the reported interval for the great majority of samples, and predictive uncertainty visibly widens toward the extremes of the observed range - behavior qualitatively consistent with what is expected of a Gaussian process's predictive uncertainty. We report these as predictive uncertainty estimates rather than as calibrated confidence levels, since a formal calibration assessment (e.g., a coverage or reliability analysis) was not separately performed in this study.

The consistently lower R^2 for D across every model warrants explanation: D=L_c-L_e is the small difference of two much larger, highly correlated quantities, so it inherits the absolute noise of both L_c and L_e while its own signal is an order of magnitude smaller than either - a harder regression problem by construction, not evidence of a modeling deficiency. We report this plainly instead of presenting only the strongest target's results, consistent with the transparency this paper's contributions are built on.

VI-B. Model Selection
------------------------------------------------------------
Ranking each of the seven models by average rank across mean-of-folds MAE, RMSE, and R^2 (Section IV-E) selects Gaussian process regression for all three targets, with an average rank of 1.0 in every case: GPR achieved the strongest overall performance across the evaluated metrics and targets in this experimental setup, ranking first on every individual metric for every target under this study's specific cross-validation protocol, dataset, and hyperparameter search. This result reflects performance on the evaluated 96-point dataset and should not be read as implying GPR's superiority under other datasets or experimental conditions. At n<=96, Gaussian process regression's characteristic O(n^3) training cost is trivial (well under 0.1 s per fold) and inference completes in well under one millisecond per sample, so the scalability caveat typically associated with GPR does not apply at this problem size.

------------------------------------------------------------

Two findings emerge, both reported without favoring the more flattering interpretation. First, switching from a mean-of-folds statistic (R^2=0.406) to the pooled statistic that the reference study appears to use (R^2=0.508) for the same random forest model closes a substantial fraction of the originally observed gap, confirming that part of the discrepancy owes to evaluation-statistic mismatch and not to model quality. We regard this as an honestly reported, partially unresolved discrepancy rather than a claim of exact replication.

VI-D. Effect of Genuine XGBoost Implementation
------------------------------------------------------------
An initial development pass, conducted in a network-restricted environment, substituted GradientBoostingRegressor for XGBoost wherever the genuine library could not be installed, with the substitution explicitly logged. On installing the genuine XGBRegressor library and re-running the identical, seed-fixed pipeline, the real implementation achieved marginally lower cross-validation R^2 than the substitute across all three targets (e.g., R^2(L_c) of 0.953 versus 0.978 for the substitute). We attribute this to the single-target hyperparameter configuration (Section IV-C) generalizing slightly differently between the two libraries' distinct hyperparameter spaces at this small sample size, and not to any implementation defect, and note that this substitution did not alter which model was ultimately selected for deployment (Gaussian process regression won regardless of which boosting implementation was compared against it). We report this counter-intuitive result in full instead of omitting it, consistent with this paper's standard of reporting what was measured, not what was expected.

VI-E. Explainability and Cross-Study Comparison
------------------------------------------------------------
Figure 5 shows SHAP summary plots for the random forest model on all three targets. Restricting the feature set to the five raw set-points (to match the reference study's input space, which contained no engineered features), our SHAP ranking for D is T_2 > T_4 > T_5 > T_3 > T_1. The reference study reports, for their equivalent random forest SHAP analysis, T_2 > T_4 > T_3 > T_1 > T_5. The top two SHAP-ranked features agree exactly between two independently trained models on two different data splits: both rank the upper heater (T_2) and lower heater (T_4) as the leading contributors to interface deflection by a wide margin over the remaining three heaters. The bottom three heaters reorder between the two studies, but both agree they are minor contributors - consistent with fold-to-fold noise on a genuinely small effect, not a substantive disagreement between the two SHAP rankings. For interface position, the reference study finds T_2 strongest, "followed by T_3 and T_4," with T_1 and T_5 markedly weaker; our raw-feature rankings for L_c (T_2 > T_3 > T_4 > T_1 > T_5) and L_e (T_3 > T_2 > T_1 > T_5 > T_4) again place the same trio (T_2, T_3, T_4) ahead of the same pair (T_1, T_5), reproducing their ranking independently. We emphasize that this is a comparison of SHAP-derived feature-importance rankings between two independently trained models, not an experimental or physical validation of causal mechanism: SHAP attributes relative contribution within a fitted statistical model, and agreement between two such rankings corroborates the model-level finding without, on its own, establishing the underlying physical cause.

Beyond this comparison, our engineered features reveal signal that a raw-feature-only input space cannot access: the overall mean furnace temperature T and the zone 1-2 gradient Delta T_12 rank above every individual raw heater temperature for both L_c and L_e, and Delta T_12 ranks third for D, ahead of three of the five raw temperatures. This demonstrates that the aggregate thermal level and local axial gradient carry explanatory information beyond what any single set-point conveys in isolation - a structural finding the reference study's feature representation could not have surfaced.

VI-H. Virtual Candidate Exploration Results
------------------------------------------------------------
Using the fixed, already-trained Gaussian process surrogate with no retraining, we sampled N=1000 candidate heater set-point combinations uniformly within the observed per-heater training ranges (Table 6), subject to the physical ordering constraint. All 1000 requested candidates were obtained on the first sampling pass (zero shortfall). Every one of the 1000 candidates satisfied all three support checks described in Section IV-J - in-range, ordering-valid, and inside the convex hull of the training design - so the entire candidate set was categorized in_range, with zero candidates flagged outside_range or outside_convex_hull. Under a uniform sampling strategy bounded to the training ranges, this indicates that the convex-hull check added no further discriminating power beyond the simpler range and ordering checks for this particular dataset and sampling strategy: the original factorial design already spans the extreme corners of the feasible region densely enough that interior uniform samples rarely fall outside its hull. We report this as an honest, dataset-specific finding, not evidence that convex-hull screening is unnecessary in general; a sparser or more irregular candidate design could behave differently, and the check remains implemented and reported for that reason.

VI-I. Towards Human-in-the-Loop Decision Support
------------------------------------------------------------
The result of Section VI-H is not a single recommended set-point but a reviewable table: 1000 evaluated candidates, each carrying a prediction, an uncertainty estimate, a support category, and a screening score, exportable in three complementary ranked views. This is a direct, systematic generalization of the reference study's own decision-support step, which consisted of manually reading a SHAP plot and checking four hand-picked candidate adjustments against scenarios already present in the training data. Where that process could examine only as many candidates as a person chooses to construct by hand, this module surrogate-evaluates and screens a thousand at negligible computational cost, explicitly separating candidates that are well supported by the training design from those that are not.

We are careful not to overstate this contribution: what this paper delivers is the candidate generation, evaluation, filtering, and ranking pipeline and its output tables, not the outcome of an actual human-expert review session. No domain expert's assessment of these specific candidates is reported here, and none is claimed. The intended workflow - a human reviewer using Table 7 and its companion views to select a small number of candidates for CrysMAS re-simulation or physical trial - is described as a design goal of the system in Section V, not as a result that has already been carried out.

==============================================================================
VII. LIMITATIONS
==============================================================================
This study's conclusions should be interpreted with several limitations in mind. The underlying data consists of only 96 samples, generated entirely by CrysMAS numerical simulation, not physical measurement; every reported number describes model fit to that simulation's output at these 96 sampled operating points, not validated agreement with a real furnace, and the model has not been validated against experimental crystal-growth data of any kind. This sample size is small for a seven-model comparison - the effective training set, after the 80/20 split, is approximately 77 rows - and cross-validation statistics - particularly for the deflection target D - carry substantial fold-to-fold variance (standard deviations up to 0.27 in R^2), meaning single-seed results should be interpreted as indicative rather than precise. The single-target hyperparameter tuning simplification (Section IV-C) was adopted for stability at this sample size but is not equivalent to a fully nested search. The pooled-versus-reference-study R^2 discrepancy for D (Section VI-C) is only partially resolved and its exact remaining cause is not identified. No governing heat-transfer equations are encoded in the surrogate itself, which remains purely data-driven rather than physics-informed. Finally, the GPR surrogate's predictive standard deviations are reported and used throughout this paper as model-reported, uncalibrated uncertainty estimates: no formal calibration or coverage-validation study (e.g., checking whether the reported intervals achieve their nominal coverage rate on held-out data) was performed, so these values should not be read as statistically calibrated confidence levels.

The candidate exploration module inherits every limitation of the underlying surrogate: its predictions, uncertainty estimates, and support flags are only as trustworthy as the GPR model they come from, and none of its 1000 evaluated candidates has been independently verified against CrysMAS or a physical trial - they were sampled and evaluated only by the trained surrogate and were never simulated or physically tested. For this specific dataset and uniform sampling strategy, convex-hull screening did not exclude any candidate beyond what range and ordering checks already excluded (Section VI-H); this finding should not be generalized to other sampling strategies or datasets without re-checking. The screening-score weights (w_D=0.5, w_u=0.3, w_c=0.2) are a documented but not empirically or physically validated choice, and a different weighting would produce a different ranking. The aggregate-uncertainty and screening-score values are normalized within a single exploration run and are not comparable across runs with different candidate sets. Finally, "human-in-the-loop decision support" in this paper describes the design and output format of the candidate table intended for expert review (Section IV-K); human expert review of these outputs was not conducted, and no natural-language explanation layer beyond the SHAP plots and numeric tables reported here has been implemented or evaluated as part of this work.

==============================================================================
VIII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
==============================================================================
This paper presented an uncertainty-aware machine learning framework for surrogate modeling and decision support in melt-crystal interface control for a five-heater vertical Bridgman furnace, directly extending the only prior machine-learning study on this exact simulation dataset. By independently modeling three physically distinct interface quantities across seven algorithms under rigorous, leakage-free cross-validation, we identified Gaussian process regression as the model that achieved the strongest overall performance across the evaluated metrics and targets in this experimental setup, additionally supplying predictive uncertainty estimates absent from all prior work on this system. By introducing a pooled cross-validation statistic matched to the reference study's own apparent evaluation methodology, we resolved a substantial fraction of an initially observed performance discrepancy while honestly reporting what remained unresolved. By comparing our SHAP-derived feature rankings against the reference study's independently derived SHAP findings, we observed cross-study agreement on the leading heater variables associated with interface deflection, while demonstrating that engineered thermal-gradient features carry signal the reference study's raw-feature representation could not access. Finally, by extending a single manual heater-adjustment procedure into both an automated, uncertainty-penalized single-target optimizer and a systematic, constraint-filtered, uncertainty-flagged candidate exploration pipeline, we demonstrated a reusable, reviewable path from surrogate model to a structured set of process recommendations designed for human expert judgement.

Future work should prioritize closing the limitations identified in Section VII: expanding the underlying simulation design of experiments to reduce cross-validation variance; validating optimizer- and exploration-recommended set-points against CrysMAS re-simulation or physical trials; conducting an actual human-expert review study of the candidate exploration module's output tables, rather than only reporting their design; and incorporating governing heat-transfer relationships as a physics-informed prior - for instance, as a structured mean function within the Gaussian process - to move from a purely data-driven surrogate toward a hybrid digital twin.
